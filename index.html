AI adoption meets high scale production

Ruli Weisbach/EVP of R&D, AppsFlyer
Welcome. The boat is AppsFlyer AI and the wave is our scale: 400 billion events a day. Everyone here knows AI can write code. This talk is about the part after: getting AI-written code safely into a production system that processes about 400 billion events a day. Four parts: who we are and our scale, the AI mania and how far it took us, the bottlenecks we hit, and how we move forward under that reality.
Before AppsFlyer

Big spend. No trace.

Before AppsFlyer, brands invested heavily in marketing for their mobile apps, but could not tell which channel or which ad actually brought them users. On the web you have cookies, so there is a trail from the ad to the purchase. On mobile, a user sees an ad, goes to the app store, downloads the app, and there is no trace of where they came from. Money goes in on one side, installs come out on the other, and nobody knows which channel worked.
After AppsFlyer

Every ad. Every install. Connected.

With AppsFlyer we solve it from both ends. All the media sources, our partners, send us a signal every time someone sees or clicks an ad. And our SDK runs inside the app, so we know the moment a user installs. We match each install back to the ad that led to it, so the brand sees exactly which channel and which ad brought the users. We are integrated this way with more than 10,000 partners. Bar lengths are illustrative.

Under the hood

Architecture

Now I am going to share how our architecture looks. Think of it as a city: Kafka, Airflow, AWS EC2 and EMR, Aerospike, DynamoDB and Kubernetes are the buildings, and about 400 billion events a day are the traffic between them, across roughly a thousand services. Every change an AI agent writes has to land safely in this city.
At this size

A small bug is never small

Someone asked me two years ago: what happens if you lose a million events? I said, what happens if you wake up with a scratchy throat? A million is nothing at this scale. The flip side is that a small bug reaches billions of events within minutes. That is the environment AI-written code has to land in.

Project 9000: we took three hard problems that, without AI, were each estimated at months of work for one engineer. We gave each problem one engineer and three months, with AI only and full tooling. Three problems times one engineer times three months is a 9 on paper; the name 9000 is the bet that with AI it would not be 9, it would be something of a different order. And it worked: we showed we can code them easily with AI. Moving attribution from in-memory to batch processing had been postponed for four years, merging three platforms into one replaced a fragmented ingestion system across 20 networks, and the Neo4j to Postgres migration had been postponed for five years. Part 03

Bottlenecks

Part three. Then we hit the bottlenecks. Coding got fast, and everything after it did not: the AI-written code arrived at the same wall of testing, rollout to production, monitoring, incidents and design decisions.
The trap

10× the code is not 10× the value

Something I read the morning of the recording: 10x faster coding does not mean 10x faster productivity and does not mean 10x better success. It can mean the opposite. If developers suddenly produce ten times the code, you can get ten times the bugs, incidents and unhappy customers, including customers annoyed that the product changes every two days. Bottleneck 1 · Design

The long loop

The first bottleneck is the back-and-forth between product and R&D. Product comes with an initial spec, R&D starts the design, and three weeks later we find the spec is not feasible, or needs a huge refactor. One recent project took over a year and a half for these reasons. With around a thousand services, nobody can sit for a day and know what a change touches. This is what Track 1 attacks: use AI early, scanning all our repos, to estimate which services a requirement touches and how complex it is, before anyone commits.
Bottleneck 2 · Testing

Proof takes weeks

● 90% of the rows match AI "Good enough." Human "Show me the other 10%."
On the merge-three-platforms project, implementation took one or two days per network and verification took weeks. Testing and verification were consistently the longest phase. The AI is also over-optimistic about its own results: it reported that 90 percent of cost rows matched and called it good enough, and a person had to push for the rest. That is why humans define the test scope. Track 3 helps: agents already propose and fix unit tests and CI failures on pull requests, and create production-grade test data in dev environments. Bar lengths are illustrative.
Bottleneck 3 · Monitoring

Which change caused the incident?

With AI coding, engineers generate more merge requests, faster, and push much more to production. Before AI, a handful of deploys went out; when a metric spiked you could line it up with the one change that went out just before it. Now dozens of changes reach production in the same window, and when something breaks or a graph moves, it is hard to understand which change caused it. We also measure adoption, meaning whether customers actually use what we shipped, and watch production behavior, but more changes in flight makes attribution harder. The pressure shifts from writing code to integration and production, and it shows up as incidents that we then have to handle. Chart is illustrative.

Moving forward

This is where the story turns. Only after we hit the wall did we realize that coding is one step in the SDLC and that we had to improve all of it, so we invest across the lifecycle: spec to design, AI coding, testing and environment provisioning, and CI/CD with AI-native pipelines. Under all four sits a common foundation. First, a clear mapped view of our architecture, services and code, rebuilt every night from the repos and exposed to agents; having it is vital for every step of the SDLC, because an agent that does not know our system cannot design, code, test or ship safely. Second, a shared marketplace of skills, tools and MCPs, so every team builds on the same capabilities instead of reinventing them. And then we started to measure value and ROI.
Context

Give the agent the map

The hardest part for the attribution team was not writing code. It was learning the platform: BigQuery, the logical data layer, deployment rules, with zero prior context. Broad questions got confident but wrong answers; specific questions worked. AF Context is our answer: a catalog and dependency graph of services, who owns them, what they talk to, where the code lives, rebuilt every night and exposed to agents through one MCP server. In a demo, Cursor used it to visualize the Kafka topology of a team, grouped by cluster, tracking messages from producers to topics to consumers.

Burning tokens is burning money, not proof the effort points the right way. I know CEOs who watch dashboards of tokens used and think more is better. At AppsFlyer we look at classic metrics, cycle time, merge requests, plan versus actual and the cumulative flow, and then correlate their trend with how much AI a group consumes. Not per person. When a group of 30 engineers raises AI consumption, does cycle time shrink? Does customer adoption rise? Often you find no correlation, or a new bottleneck in integration or production. It is a question to steer by, not a KPI.

Under this reality, the first rule is to pick bets with a high, clear return. Six concrete examples. Infrastructure migration: moving from Neo4j to a relational database, done in Project 9000. Logic transformation: moving attribution from coded logic to SQL logic for batch processing, also Project 9000. Data deletion: turning the manual GDPR process into an agentic one. Test data generation: generating test data and spinning up a test environment per feature, which attacks the testing bottleneck directly. Data platform navigation: generating queries based on our data platform metadata. Customer intelligence agent: Know Your Customer is an agent that gives a 360-degree view of our customers, what they ask for and what they need. What they share: the destination is known, the old way is a reference to compare against, and the effort is repetitive enough for agents to take over. When we rewrite or migrate, do it side by side and ramp gradually; at HP Mercury a big-bang rewrite with zero customers collapsed after a year, and AI makes big bang more tempting because building new is so cheap. Resist it.
The take

Build windmills

My take on the AI revolution, a little banal: when the storm comes, some hide and some build windmills. It is enormous power, and with great power you need to know how to channel it. Companies that channel it into being more productive and creating more value for their customers will succeed. Companies that misuse it will burn. The signs of success I look for in a year: do we understand much faster whether an idea is feasible, is production more stable, and do our customers get more value.
